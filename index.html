Slide 1: Welcome & Day 1 Scope

Welcome to English 11! Today, we begin constructing our conceptual framework for "American Identity" by examining indigenous oral narratives and foundational cosmogonic myths.


Core Objective: Evaluate creation accounts to extract underlying communal principles and societal ethics.

Slide 2: The OTIS Framework

Reading complex texts requires an intentional strategy. Use OTIS to guide your analysis:


  • O — Observe: Slow down and look closely at textual details without pre-judging.
  • T — Think: Ask critical questions about patterns, word choice, and structure.
  • I — Interpret: Connect evidence to broader cultural themes and intentions.
  • S — Synthesize: Combine insights to form grounded, evidence-based conclusions.

Slide 3: Literary Context & Chronology

Literature does not exist in a vacuum. Before diving into texts, we use Mr. Boehm's Handy Handout on Literary Time Periods to locate where our readings sit historically.


Key Concept: Note how readings published outside their historical eras can still align with the themes and values of past movements.

Slide 4: Era Focus — Native Era vs. Colonial Era

  • The Native Era (Before 1620 CE): Rich oral traditions, cosmogonic myths, deep ecological relationships, and communal governance.
  • The Puritan / Colonial Era (1620–1750 CE): Written records, theological prose, ascetic aesthetics, and frontier narratives.

Discussion Anchor: How does transitioning from oral storytelling to written typography alter the way history and culture are preserved?

Slide 5: The Power of Oral Tradition

Oral narratives are active, evolving safeguards of cultural heritage. They establish:


  • Origin motifs and human origins.
  • Ecological harmony and environmental stewardship.
  • Communal ethics and societal governance.

Slide 6: Case Study 1 — Iroquois Cosmology

Text Focus: "The World on the Turtle’s Back"


  • Key Motif: Balance between opposing forces (the right-handed and left-handed twins).
  • OTIS Observation Goal: Notice how creation involves collaboration between celestial beings and earthly animals.
  • Textual Focus: How does the text establish accountability to the natural world?

Slide 7: Case Study 2 — Ojibwe Lore

Text Focus: "Manabozho and the Muskrat"


  • Key Motif: Persistence, humility, and sacrifice for the greater community.
  • Comparative Question: How does the small muskrat's role challenge traditional European definitions of heroism?

Slide 8: Case Study 3 — Diné Emergence

Text Focus: Hajíínéí: The Navajo Account of Ascent


  • Key Motif: Sequential progression through distinct worlds to achieve cosmic harmony.
  • Structural Focus: Reviewing the phased journey from lower realms to spiritual and physical completion.

Slide 12: Group Discourse & Socratic Prompts

Seminar Discussion Questions:


  • 1. Why is oral tradition an effective mechanism for safeguarding societal values?
  • 2. How do indigenous creation myths differ from European Enlightenment perspectives on human dominion over nature?

Slide 13: Lesson Synthesis

  • American literature begins long before European colonization with diverse indigenous oral traditions.
  • Cosmogonic myths serve as foundational texts that define societal ethics, governance, and ecological stewardship.
  • Using frameworks like OTIS prevents us from making rushed or biased assumptions about unfamiliar cultural texts.
