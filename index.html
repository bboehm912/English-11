2. Anishinaabe Orality & Sacred Narrative Transmission

MN ELA 11.1.1.1
  • Primary Text Focus: Analyzing Manabozho and the Muskrat as a foundational Anishinaabe creation account.
  • Epistemological Orality: Understanding oral performance as a dynamic, living repository of tribal jurisprudence, ecological science, and communal ethics.
  • Mnemonic Architecture: How rhythm, repetition, and spatial geography preserve complex historical memory across generations without written codification.
Critical Analysis Note: Oral texts are not primitive predecessors to written books; they are sophisticated, adaptable pedagogical instruments designed for communal endurance.

3. Cosmogony & The Earth-Diver Archetype

MN ELA 11.1.3.3
  • The Earth-Diver Motif: A prevalent mythic structure where creation emerges from primordial floodwaters through animal agency.
  • Selflessness vs. Heroism: In Manabozho and the Muskrat, creation succeeds not through the physical strength of mighty creatures, but through the sacrificial perseverance of the humble Muskrat (*Waizhuck*).
  • Reciprocal Ecology: Soil placed upon the Turtle's back demonstrates the interdependence between human life and animal sacrifice.
Textual Focus Question: Why does Ojibwe cosmogony honor the smallest creature above the largest predators? What cultural values are imparted?

4. Comparative Worldviews: Eco-Centrism vs. Dominion

MN ELA 11.1.9.9
  • Kinship Model: Indigenous oral traditions treat non-human elements (animals, winds, waters) as moral actors and elder relatives.
  • Euro-American Tradition: Contrast with Judeo-Christian Genesis, which posits human dominion over nature and a hierarchical creation ladder.
  • Rhetorical Implications: How initial cosmological assumptions shape centuries of land use, environmental policy, and literary allegory.

5. The American Literary Continuum & Time Periods

MN ELA 11.1.2.2
  • Reference Text: Integrating our master reference document Literary Time Periods.
  • Chronological Epochs: Tracking literature from Native Orality (Pre-1600) $\rightarrow$ Colonial/Puritan (1620–1750) $\rightarrow$ Revolutionary/Enlightenment (1750–1800) $\rightarrow$ Transcendentalism (1830–1865) $\rightarrow$ Realism $\rightarrow$ Modernism.
  • Historiography Critique: Examining how traditional anthologies historically excluded oral traditions from the official "canon."
Methodology Challenge: How does classifying oral literature within a linear European timeline alter or restrict our understanding of its true cultural timelessness?

6. Colonial First Encounters & Rhetorical Framing

MN ELA 11.2.4.4
  • The Contact Zone: Analyzing journals from Columbus, Cabeza de Vaca, and John Smith.
  • Rhetoric of Justification: How early European writers employed biblical typography and "wilderness" metaphors to rationalize territorial expansion.
  • Captivity Narratives: Dissecting Mary Rowlandson’s narrative as both psychological defense and early American bestseller.

7. Transcendentalism & Reclaiming the Natural Sacred

MN ELA 11.1.4.4
  • Emerson & Thoreau: 19th-century intellectuals seeking divine immanence in nature after industrial urbanization.
  • Unintended Parallels: How Transcendentalist concepts like the "Over-Soul" echoed Indigenous eco-philosophy, yet remained rooted in European individualism.
  • Civil Disobedience: The ethical mandate to prioritize moral truth over institutional law.

8. Introducing The O.T.I.S. Analytical Framework

Core Academic Methodology
  • O - Objectivity: Isolating textual claims and cultural facts from modern reader projection or implicit bias.
  • T - Truthfulness: Evaluating authorial motivation, historical context, and internal narrative consistency.
  • I - Integrity: Demonstrating academic scholarship through precise textual citation and respectful critique.
  • S - Stewardship: Preserving the dignity of diverse cultural narratives and engaging in ethical classroom dialogue.
Framework Standard: All essay assignments and worksheet responses in this course will be evaluated using the O.T.I.S. rubric criteria.

9. Media Literacy: Mythmaking in Modern Media

MN ELA 11.3.1.1

  • Archetype Identification: Recognizing the Earth-Diver, the Trickster, and the Sacrificial Hero in modern media narratives.
  • Critical Deconstruction: Analyzing media formats to reveal embedded ideological assumptions and power structures.
